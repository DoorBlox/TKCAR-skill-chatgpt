# Art direction, asset density, reference research, and MM handoff

## 1. Translate the concept to a visual grammar

Define six layers before collecting hundreds of references:

1. **Hero subject:** what occupies the center of key visuals; could be place, artifact, mechanism, social scene, or character.
2. **Formal language:** angular/soft, ornate/minimal, organic/mechanical, symmetrical/asymmetric, flat/deep.
3. **Material and architecture:** what objects appear to be made from; functional spaces, not only ornament.
4. **Color system:** shared base colors, category accents, hierarchy, adequate legibility; not just isolated hex codes.
5. **Light/time/mood:** bright/festive, dusk/mysterious, theatrical, airy, documentary, etc. Choose strategically rather than applying every mood.
6. **Graphic system:** symbols, framing, typography character, cartographic elements, diagrams, image treatments, patterns.

“Fantasy,” “magical,” “epic,” or “aesthetic” alone are not art direction. Name *how the image will look* and what the MM team can consistently repeat.

## 2. Shared DNA versus sub-identity

Build a two-level vocabulary:

**Global (invariant):** material family, illustration treatment, baseline lighting, proportions, shared pattern/emblem structure, graphic overlay rules, typography hierarchy.

**Local (variable):** individual silhouette, accent colors, shape direction, architectural functions, signature objects, mood intensity, patterns, perhaps soundtrack/motion cue.

Check identity in **grayscale** and at small scale. Can categories still be distinguished beyond accent color? Check cohesion side-by-side. Would a viewer infer one underlying franchise/world?

## 3. Controlled palette planning

A useful default is:

- 2–3 shared background/neutral colors.
- 1–2 shared luminous/metallic/emphasis colors if relevant.
- 1–2 accents per sub-identity.

This is a starting structure, *not* a universal requirement; MM may produce a better system. Colors must serve clear functional hierarchy and accessibility, especially text contrast and outdoor stage legibility. Avoid telling MM that every surface must be a field's accent color.

## 4. Asset-density stress test

Test at least **eight** representative deliverables, or all available ones if fewer. Example mix:

| Deliverable | What it tests |
|---|---|
| Main hero poster | Signature anchor and compositional hierarchy |
| Category announcement | Distinct identities under one system |
| Social-media carousel | Variety without losing unity |
| Teaser animation keyframes | Narrative progression and motion motifs |
| Event stage backdrop | Legibility, scale, production practicality |
| Physical corridor/decor | Shape simplification and budget |
| Signage/map | Wayfinding usability and cross-category consistency |
| Mascot stickers/merch | Character silhouette and expression |
| Countdown template | Repeatable content without exhaustion |
| Certificate/ticket | Formal use in low-detail format |

For each, ask: What *unique content* comes from the event world? Does it require unrelated generic props? Can it be produced and read at its intended scale? Which design challenges should MM validate?

If an idea performs poorly, first try enriching the central anchor (rooms, roles, tools, rituals, symbols, transformations). Expand the world only if the concept requires it.

## 5. Pinterest/reference research protocol

A strong search packet contains, per category:

- **3–5 target attributes:** e.g., vertical towers, layered fabrics, mechanical astronomy, kinetic crowd scenes.
- **5–10 direct search queries:** mix franchise/film/game, environment concept art, architecture and object terms, art techniques.
- **Reference transfer annotation:** “Use: monumental ship silhouette / Avoid: exact character and crest.”
- **Relevance to execution:** what it could inspire: poster, backdrop, mascot, prop, costume, typography.

Don't overuse one fandom or build sub-identities as “Sci-Tech = Franchise A / Journalism = Franchise B.” Explain a specific property in each reference. Confirm factual reference names where necessary; do not invent scenes or claim a source has certain visuals without checking when accuracy matters.

### Search-query recipes

- `<specific film/game> environment concept art`
- `<visual property> fantasy architecture`
- `<architectural function> art direction`
- `<setting> <lighting> illustration`
- `<character anatomy> expressive mascot design`
- `<fabric/material> stylized concept art`
- `<motif> graphic system posters`
- `<category> festival stage design`

Franchises are references, not creative ownership. Avoid direct recreation of protected characters, logos, crests, and recognizable distinctive designs.

## 6. The MM feedback loop

Hold a quick joint concept review **before** locking visuals:

1. TKCAR explains the premise, boundaries, required fields, intended emotion, and why chosen motifs matter.
2. MM restates the concept in its own words and identifies underdefined parts.
3. Both rank risky applications (e.g., 3D decor, motion, character animation, category subbrands).
4. MM proposes interpretation and alternative references, including what doesn't work.
5. TKCAR resolves *conceptual* changes; MM resolves *visual/technical* specifics.
6. Record decisions, unresolved risks, mockup ownership, and next review.

Ask not “Can MM do it?” in the abstract, but “Which of these applications is the bottleneck and why?”

## 7. Handoff wording: weak versus actionable

Weak:

> This is a pirate theme. Please include compasses, ropes, lamps, treasure and waves, and make four areas different.

Actionable:

> The event world is organized around four communities connected by navigation. Each area is a self-contained inhabited vessel, not merely a recolored ship. Their shapes, spaces, and symbols should express four distinct functions while sharing the same material and atmospheric vocabulary. Generic sea imagery is support, not the hero subject. MM may reinterpret the suggested references, but should preserve this hierarchy and bring back a visual-system test for the four categories.

This is a **demonstration of instruction quality**, not a recommended theme for new events.

## 8. Decoration and physical execution

Mockups must account for actual human routes, crowd safety, fabrication time, assembly, storage, cost, power, venue permissions, and weather if outdoors. Sometimes a world concept needs to become a **flat layered set, lightweight facade, modular sign, or lighting cue** rather than full-scale sculptural architecture. Art direction should be scalable from elaborate illustrations to feasible physical props.

## 9. Accessibility and audience reality

Themed lettering, glitter, dark atmospheres, and complex visual lore may undermine legibility. Preserve readable contrast, clear schedules, accessible directional signage, and a strong information hierarchy. A good promotional visual still communicates what, when, where, and why an event matters.

## 10. Visual QA checklist

- Can viewers identify the hero subject without reading lore?
- Does each field have a distinct silhouette *and* recognizable global DNA?
- Is the style repeatable by different MM members?
- Can at least eight meaningful assets be derived without decorative randomization?
- Are typography and information layers readable at expected sizes?
- Can physical designs fit budget, labor, venue, and safety requirements?
- Are all references transformed rather than copied?
- Has MM explicitly reviewed these assumptions?
