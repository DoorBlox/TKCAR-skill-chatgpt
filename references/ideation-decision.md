# Divergent ideation, comparison, and decision science

## 1. Prevent premature aesthetic commitment

Starting with a lush title, character, or Pinterest board biases everyone toward elaborating the first attractive image. Begin with *world mechanism*, not poetic naming:

- What is the central phenomenon, premise, or tension?
- What constitutes the world (and what is mere background)?
- What must the audience do, witness, or discover?
- What makes this specifically relevant to the event's categories?
- What could produce multiple cohesive design assets?

Generate **three structurally different routes** when the user asks for alternatives. Different names or mood colors do not count as different routes. Possible route archetypes (choose relevant ones; never a prescribed theme):

1. **Bounded districts/realms** united by a shared law or origin.
2. **One traveling protagonist** encountering distinct stations, each causally important.
3. **One transforming shared structure** whose components represent fields.
4. **Multiple factions/voices** negotiating an alliance or creating something together.
5. **Festival of disciplines** sharing one tangible institution rather than fantasy quest lore.
6. **Real-world metaphor** designed for a contemporary/no-lore event.

Do not force fantasy lore where an elegant symbolic visual concept would fit better.

## 2. Equal-depth option card

For each route provide approximately the same detail:

- **Core premise (1–2 sentences).**
- **Signature visual anchor:** what a viewer remembers without generic décor.
- **Boundaries:** what *is* and *isn't* inside the concept.
- **Category representation:** why the required fields are distinct yet unified.
- **Narrative or audience experience:** action and progression, not a poetic paragraph.
- **Asset opportunities:** 5–8 genuinely different items or environments.
- **Biggest weak spot:** the most consequential, credible risk.
- **Minimum practical test:** quickest way to disconfirm that it can work.

If one idea gets 4 pages and the alternative gets a sentence, the comparison is invalid. Normalize presentation effort first.

## 3. First-pass knockout conditions

A route should be revised or eliminated if it:

- Violates a *confirmed* requirement without explicit authorization to change it.
- Contradicts the actual function/age/audience of the event.
- Requires a core physical implementation obviously outside available capacity.
- Cannot explain how mandatory fields or program elements belong.
- Is essentially a copyrighted franchise, derivative set of recognizable characters, or plagiarized world.

The user can still request wild ideas at a purely exploratory stage; label noncompliant routes as *speculative* rather than presenting them as approval-ready.

## 4. Weighted comparison (adaptable)

Use when there are 2–5 serious candidates and the user needs a decision. Scores: 1 weak, 2 limited, 3 workable, 4 strong, 5 exceptional. Weight can be negotiated; default weights sum to 100:

| Dimension | Default weight | Strong performance means... |
|---|---:|---|
| Coherence and internal logic | 18 | Premise, story, world, symbolism actually reinforce each other |
| Distinctiveness and originality | 14 | Recognizable apart from genre conventions and references |
| Asset density within boundaries | 14 | Many unique, coherent visual/physical outputs |
| Category fit and differentiation | 12 | Mandatory fields each have a meaningful, unified identity |
| Participant relevance and emotional effect | 10 | Inviting, comprehensible, aligned with event objectives |
| MM implementation feasibility | 10 | Practical for visual design team; not an assertion until discussed |
| Physical execution feasibility | 10 | Stage/decor/production fit current constraints |
| Narrative or experiential progression | 7 | Coherent development, transitions, reveal or engagement |
| Stakeholder requirement alignment | 5 | Matches legitimate preferences/constraints |

**Weighted score = Σ(weight × rating ÷ 5)**, on a 0–100 scale. Score ranges are *discussion prompts*, not hard scientific cutoffs. Identify confidence and missing evidence. Never turn a subjective rating into fake precision.

Include at least one **failure mode** and one **testable unknown** for the highest-scoring route. If no alternatives are viable, say so and explain repairs rather than inventing competition.

## 5. Typical faulty claims and proper tests

| Claim | Problem | Test |
|---|---|---|
| “The ocean is huge, so the concept is too broad.” | Confuses setting size and conceptual scope | Name the actual relevant anchors and excluded features |
| “A ship theme only has ships and waves.” | Underestimates genre vocabulary | Inventory unique architecture, rituals, symbols, tools, spaces, characters |
| “Any object from the genre makes a cohesive asset.” | A genre is not a design system | Compare 8 different mock briefs under one visual grammar |
| “The four areas are different because they have four colors.” | Color alone often lacks narrative/shape distinctiveness | Silhouette-only comparison, grayscale identification |
| “MM will struggle.” | Predicts others' capabilities without evidence | Ask MM; prototype 2–3 demanding deliverables |
| “Our new concept has a better storyline.” | Story may not matter equally to all stakeholders | Show how story improves campaign, design, participant journey |
| “They accepted our idea, so it is mandatory.” | Confuses enthusiasm and authorization | Check decision owner and recorded scope |
| “The new idea is better because it's richer.” | May hide implementation costs | Compare physical complexity and scale at equal fidelity |

## 6. Bias control

- **Sunk-cost bias:** Reject “we've already named it” as sufficient reason to keep it.
- **Novelty bias:** Don't prefer a new world merely because the old one feels familiar.
- **Reference bias:** A polished film still makes an unfinished idea seem better; compare concepts, not production budgets.
- **Authority bias:** A senior's view deserves consideration, not automatic acceptance.
- **Alliance bias:** Support from a respected ally does not establish correctness.
- **Narrative bias:** A beautifully written paragraph can hide plot gaps.
- **Complexity bias:** More lore does not automatically mean more coherent design.

## 7. Recommendation style

Finish a comparison with:

> **Recommend [route]** because [two decisive criteria]. **Tradeoff:** [real drawback]. **Condition:** [decision/assumption that could overturn it]. **Next test:** [an MM/production test or stakeholder clarification].

Avoid blanket declarations such as “perfect,” “flawless,” or “everyone will love it.”
